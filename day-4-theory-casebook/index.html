Case 01 · New York City · July 13, 1977

The city that stopped

At 9:36 on a hot Wednesday night, lightning hit power equipment north of the city. Several lines failed in quick succession. The electric network could not handle the sudden changes, and New York City went dark. About 9 million people lost power for roughly 25 hours.

The timing made the problem worse: it was a heat wave, people were still out for the evening, and the blackout spread across a city built around powered systems. The Mets game at Shea Stadium stopped in the sixth inning. Commuters were stranded at Grand Central. Traffic signals went dark, tunnels closed when ventilation fans stopped, and elevators trapped people. The outage became a citywide problem because daily life depended on systems working together.

Neighbors and workers also had to improvise: people helped direct traffic, checked on others, and looked for ways home. The case lets sociologists ask why one failure affected so many parts of life—and how people adapted when the usual systems stopped.

Case 02 · New York City · March 25, 1911

The factory and the locked door

About 500 people worked on the eighth, ninth, and tenth floors of the Asch Building. Most were young immigrant women and girls who sewed shirtwaists, a popular blouse. Workers had organized a major strike in 1909 to demand better pay and conditions. The factory owners controlled the workplace, including access to the doors.

Near closing time on March 25, a fire started in a bin of fabric scraps on the eighth floor. It spread quickly. There was no alarm system workers could use, the fire hose did not work, and the fire escape was unsafe. Workers on the ninth floor did not know about the fire until it reached them. They tried to escape down a staircase, but a door was locked—managers had used locked doors to limit breaks and theft. One hundred forty-six workers died.

Afterward, families, unions, and reformers pushed New York to investigate workplace conditions. The city and state passed dozens of new safety laws. Sociologists can study the disaster by asking who controlled the rules, who faced the danger, and how workers organized to demand change.

Case 03 · Riceville, Iowa · April 1968

The Iowa classroom

The day after Martin Luther King Jr. was assassinated in 1968, students in Jane Elliott’s third-grade class in Riceville, Iowa, arrived with questions about racism. Elliott designed an exercise to make unfair treatment easier to notice. She sorted children by eye color and announced that one group was smarter and better than the other. The label was arbitrary: eye color did not measure a student’s ability.

But the classroom rules changed around the label. The favored group received privileges, while the other group was treated as less capable. Students began responding to one another through those new expectations. The next day, Elliott reversed which group had higher status. Years later, the students returned for the PBS documentary A Class Divided and discussed how the experience had affected them.

This case is about the small, face-to-face moments that give labels power: who is believed, who gets included, and how people act when a group is treated as “better” or “worse.” Sociologists using this lens study how people create and respond to meanings in daily interaction.
